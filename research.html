Research

My research investigates how linguistic experience shapes speech perception and language processing. I pursue two converging aims: (1) measuring the real-world linguistic experience—community-level, individualized, and developmental—that predicts how listeners adapt to unfamiliar speech, and (2) identifying the perceptual mechanisms underlying that adaptation, asking whether experience reshapes how listeners categorize speech sounds or simply changes their willingness to accept unusual pronunciations. I also investigate how cognitive load and attention constrain speech processing when listeners encounter accented speech.

Research projects

Methodological

A Triangulated Toolkit for Quantifying Linguistic Experience

Macro-level: Stable, community-level experience

Atlas-CH — Successful language comprehension requires listeners to rapidly adapt to linguistic variability, including accented speech. While a growing body of research suggests that long-term exposure to diverse linguistic input can facilitate such adaptation, characterizing the real-world experience remains a challenge. We introduce a novel geolocation-based metric that quantifies local linguistic diversity using US Census data at the zip code level. We report an initial application of this method in a large-scale online perceptual study (N=647) of monolingual English speakers across the United States, showing that our census-based metric predicted both subjective familiarity with the accent and perceptual adaptation during the first few moments of exposure. Crucially, the effect was accent-specific: community prevalence of Chinese-speaking residents predicted adaptation to Chinese-accented English, but not to other accents, suggesting that the perceptual system is tuned by distributional properties of the specific accent rather than by general linguistic diversity.

Atlas-SP — Extending the Accent Atlas approach to Spanish-accented English in a new nationwide sample (N ≈ 650). Spanish-speaking communities are broadly distributed across the United States, providing greater variance in community-level exposure and a stronger test of whether the prevalence effect generalizes across linguistic communities.

Micro-level: Dynamic, individualized experience

SNAP — An Experience Sampling Method (ESM) protocol administered via smartphone (ExpiWell) that captures real-time attended and ambient linguistic encounters three times daily across one week. Deployed with heritage Mandarin-English and heritage Spanish-English bilinguals, SNAP separates accent from language exposure and active from passive overhearing—dimensions that retrospective self-report conflates. Combined with a perception-focused self-report questionnaire and the census-derived prevalence index, SNAP forms the third instrument of a triangulated toolkit designed to enable theory-driven tests of how experience shapes speech perception, language processing, and learning. The toolkit is adaptable to any research context where real-world experience is theorized to shape human cognition.

Perception

Adaptation to Nonnative Accents

Hearing Beyond Categories: General Adaptation to Nonnative Speech (Gu, Chao, & Xie, CogSci 2025; Gu, Cummings, Chao, & Xie, in preparation) — Processing spoken language draws on representations at multiple levels, from how listeners categorize individual speech sounds to how they map those sounds onto words. A central question is which of these levels can be reshaped by experience: does adaptation reshape how listeners categorize speech sounds, or does it simply change their willingness to accept unusual pronunciations? Using the Mandarin-accented /θ/–/s/ contrast as a case study, we designed a Pretest–Exposure–Posttest paradigm to distinguish these accounts. Across experiments, we systematically manipulate lexical context, exposure content, and task demands to isolate the mechanism driving short-term accent adaptation.

Processing

Error Detection in Nonnative Speech

Investigating how attentional resources are allocated when speech contains deviations across multiple linguistic domains simultaneously. This project asks whether the processing cost of nonnative-accented speech interacts with syntactic or semantic anomaly detection, and what this reveals about the architecture of real-time sentence processing.
