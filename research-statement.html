Research Statement

Human speech perception is a remarkable feat: listeners probabilistically map continuous speech onto discrete units of meaning at rates of up to 2.5 words per second, adapting in real time to variation across speakers and accents. When you meet someone with an unfamiliar accent (e.g., a colleague from Japan speaking English), you may struggle at first, but within minutes you start to understand them better. My research addresses a fundamental open question: what types and amounts of linguistic experience—the perceptual input a listener accumulates through daily encounters with speech—shape an individual’s capacity to adapt to unfamiliar speech, and more broadly, to language processing and learning? Below, I describe two arms of my research program, one focused on measuring the experience that shapes adaptation and one on identifying its mechanisms, both combining controlled experiments with naturalistic data.

Measuring the Experience That Shapes Adaptation

Listeners who receive more diverse linguistic input daily may become more adaptive, but this intuition has not been empirically tested, and more fundamentally, we do not yet know what types of experiences facilitate adaptive speech perception. To address this gap, I have taken a multi-method approach and built three tools that together capture a listener’s long-term everyday exposure, forming the core of my dissertation: (a) a geolocation-based accent-prevalence index built from U.S. Census data; (b) a smartphone-based momentary assessment survey that samples listeners’ linguistic exposure as it happens; and (c) an extensive questionnaire that captures individual variation in past and present linguistic experience.

The toolkit’s notable strength is its ability to combine objective and subjective measures: the census index captures ambient environmental exposure that listeners cannot self-report, while the survey and questionnaire capture each listener’s subjective experience. These distinctions matter because the amount and specificity of exposure (e.g., exposure to the Spanish language versus Spanish-accented English) and the nature of engagement (e.g., active interaction versus passive overhearing) can each shape speech perception differently. I have received a Dean’s Leadership Society Doctoral Dissertation Research Support Fellowship (one of 5–8 awarded annually) to develop and validate this toolkit.

In a study of 647 native-English listeners (Gu, Cutler, Xie, & Kurumada, 2026, Psychonomic Bulletin & Review), the census index predicted how well listeners processed Chinese-accented speech on first exposure, and the effect was accent-specific: Chinese-accent community prevalence predicted this processing, while Spanish-accent prevalence did not. This is early evidence against general nonnative-speech exposure as the driver, though the census measure alone cannot yet distinguish accent-specific from source-language exposure.

Ongoing work applies this toolkit to heritage Mandarin–English and Spanish–English speakers—people who grew up hearing a language at home but were educated mainly in English. Heritage speakers can dissociate accent exposure from source-language exposure: a heritage Mandarin speaker may hear substantial Mandarin at home without encountering proportional Mandarin-accented English. By assessing their personal linguistic history alongside their current daily exposure, I aim to understand how linguistic experience, identity, and the amount, type, and timing of exposure interact to shape adaptive speech perception. Pilot data suggest differential effects of language versus accent exposure, and of direct versus overheard exposure—the types of distinctions my toolkit is uniquely suited to capture. Future work will involve further developing this environment-aware research design, which ties listeners’ everyday linguistic experience to rigorous laboratory-based experimentation, and extending the toolkit to more bilingual communities and a broader range of accents.

Identifying the Mechanisms of Adaptation

The second arm addresses the perceptual mechanisms behind these adaptive changes. Processing spoken language draws on representations at multiple levels, from how listeners categorize individual speech sounds to how they map those sounds onto words. A central question is which of these levels can be reshaped by experience: does adaptation reshape how listeners categorize speech sounds, or does it simply change their willingness to accept unusual pronunciations? The distinction matters for how we model spoken word recognition: current models describe how listeners map incoming sounds onto words but include no mechanism for learning from experience, and empirical evidence remains scarce.

I have directly addressed this question by testing how monolingual English listeners adapt to a Mandarin-accented talker (Gu, Chao, & Xie, 2025, Proceedings of the Cognitive Science Society; Gu, Cummings, Chao, & Xie, in preparation). The talker produced two English sounds, /θ/ (as in think) and /s/ (as in sink), in a way that made them acoustically ambiguous, as often occurs in Mandarin-accented English. We gave different groups of listeners word contexts that pushed them toward hearing one sound or the other, then tested whether this shifted their basic perception using a task that asked listeners to identify the sound itself, with no word recognition involved. If the earlier exposure had only made listeners more willing to accept odd-sounding words, this test should have shown no effect. Instead, listeners’ perception shifted, showing that adaptation changes how they perceive the sound itself, not just how they judge words built from it. This rules out the account based on simple perceptual leniency. Our design also ruled out a common confound (repetitive stimulus exposure), strengthening the case for this interpretation.

Ongoing work extends this and asks whether sharing a talker’s native language gives listeners a head start (Bower, Gu, & Xie, in preparation). Our preliminary results suggest otherwise: Mandarin-English bilingual listeners can hear the acoustic difference between the two sounds, but this does not translate into more consistent categorization. This reframes an important question, shifting the focus from whether a shared language background helps listeners hear a contrast to whether it helps them learn it. I am conducting follow-up tests that directly compare how much each group adapts with exposure.

My future work will continue to address what exactly changes as exposure to a speech variant accumulates: are listeners updating their underlying sound categories, or just shifting how readily they decide between two sounds they can already tell apart? I plan to use computational simulation approaches to examine these contrasting theories. The perceptual categorization data I have collected from over 300 participants will be directly applicable to this direction.

These experimental approaches also have broader implications for attention, cognitive load, and memory during speech processing. Adapting to an unfamiliar accent is often automatic, yet processing accented speech is generally taxing—a split with interesting behavioral consequences. In an ongoing study (Gu, Hsu, Kaiser, & Xie, 2026 HSP poster), listeners get better at detecting ordinary speech errors (e.g., grammatical errors) in accented speech but remain insensitive to sound-level errors, suggesting that the cognitive load engaged at these two levels may be distinct. Critically, no improvement was observed in a different task, transcribing accented speech, potentially suggesting that the more automatic word recognition used in transcription diverges from more explicit error monitoring, and that the former is more closely linked to the automatic, implicit process of accent adaptation. I plan to develop more targeted experimental designs to examine limits on attention, cognitive control, and memory, with the goal of informing spoken language communication in linguistically diverse classrooms and clinical settings.

Broad Trajectory

In the next five years, my lab will continue to study how diverse linguistic environments shape speech processing across the lifespan, in three directions. First, I will apply the toolkit to a broader set of questions, such as whether everyday-exposure measures predict production behaviors like code-switching or accommodation in bilinguals (building on Song, Canales, Gu et al., 2023, Proceedings of the Cognitive Science Society). Second, I plan to apply for NSF funding to launch a large-scale study tracking the accent-adaptation trajectories of incoming students. Understanding students’ linguistic experiences during their first year is of both scientific and educational interest, since many encounter diverse linguistic input for the first time; I aim to test whether these changes in experience affect their perceptual, linguistic, and cognitive abilities. Third, I will refine the computational simulation approaches needed to distinguish the perceptual mechanisms documented behind natural accent adaptation. I also plan to build on the error monitoring findings to investigate how individual differences in attentional capacity and cognitive control shape speech processing under demanding listening conditions. All work is designed around transparent, reusable materials and data pipelines, and it offers natural entry points for undergraduate involvement at every stage.
