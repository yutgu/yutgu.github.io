Teaching Statement

Whether a student goes on to graduate school, clinical practice, or a career outside psychology, I want them to leave my courses able to evaluate a claim about human behavior, recognize the evidence behind it, and identify when that evidence falls short. In a world full of confident-sounding assertions about how people think, learn, and communicate, this is both an academic and a practical skill. As the instructor of record for a 29-student language acquisition seminar, a teaching assistant and lab instructor across six courses—probability and statistics, psychology of language, human memory, developmental psychology, experimental methods, and a seminar on current topics in speech science—and a research mentor to undergraduate students, I have organized my teaching around three practices: making the implicit norms of the discipline explicit, building complex skills through structured practice, and connecting scientific thinking to students’ own lives and goals.

Making the Implicit Explicit

Much of what determines whether a student succeeds in an academic setting depends on norms that are rarely stated outright—how to participate in discussion, when to visit office hours, how to approach a research article, how to ask for help. Having moved through the educational system in mainland China before pursuing graduate study in the United States, I experienced this gap firsthand. The classrooms I grew up in placed great value on foundational rigor and careful preparation. The seminars I entered as a graduate student ran on open debate, where forming and defending a position out loud mattered as much as arriving at the right answer. Navigating that shift gave me an analytical perspective on the distance between what institutions expect and what students have been prepared for—a distance that affects students from all backgrounds, not only those crossing national borders. I now make these expectations visible rather than assuming students already know them. I explain what office hours are for, demonstrate how to approach a research article, and treat help-seeking as a skill to be taught.

These principles shape my own development as an instructor. In my language acquisition course (summer 2026, 29 students), a student emailed to challenge a test question about constrained generalization, pointing out that the slides defined the concept in a way that made the marked answer seem wrong. The student was right that the slides had a gap. I replied explaining the reasoning, acknowledged the gap publicly, gave credit to everyone on the question, and revised the material for the review session the next day. Several students later emailed saying they felt welcomed in the course and could tell I was enthusiastic about the topic. These moments matter because they show students that questioning is productive, that the instructor can be wrong, and that the classroom runs on evidence rather than authority.

Building Complex Skills through Structured Practice

What carries forward into any career—clinical practice, human resources, or academia—is the capacity to break a complex problem into steps, tolerate uncertainty while gathering evidence, and communicate findings clearly. I aim to build these skills through structured activities that move students from guided practice to increasing independence.

Developing Research Skills

As a teaching assistant running lab sessions in probability and statistics, I moved students from copying formulas to analyzing real data. In an upper-level seminar on current topics in speech science, I designed research 101 sessions on skills that coursework tends to assume but rarely teaches directly: how to read a primary research article, how to interpret graphs, and how to decide whether a paper merits a detailed read. Students worked in study groups and presented papers to each other. Those who found this overwhelming at the start finished the term presenting published findings to their peers. One activity I found especially effective was having students record their own speech data and collect data from a couple of friends. I then plotted their responses, and students were intrigued to see their own categorization functions, which made a core perceptual phenomenon concrete in a way that a textbook figure cannot. This also cultivated their interest in collecting real human data and measuring it against theoretical predictions.

Adapting Courses to Student Needs

Not every student in a psychology course intends to become a psychologist, and recognizing this changed how I teach. In my language acquisition course, many students had clinical interests. I responded by focusing on language and cognitive development in special populations, connecting course content to career paths students were actually considering.

I also learned to adapt when a strategy was not working. I initially designed review sessions to cover all review-sheet questions, but students were not engaged. I changed the format: before each review session, students posted questions on a discussion board and attempted to answer one another’s questions, citing specific course materials including lecture notes, podcasts, and accessible articles from Scientific American that I selected for their clarity and relevance. I then organized the session around the most frequently asked themes, projected the posted questions, acknowledged the student responses, and added my own take. Students reported that this format helped them engage with material they would not have questioned on their own, and that answering peers’ questions forced them to revisit and consolidate their understanding. This format also gave quieter students a way to participate in the intellectual conversation through writing rather than speaking up in a large class. The citation requirement built the habit of sourcing claims and engaging with science communication written for non-specialists, a skill that transfers to any profession.

I also designed timed assessments where students created a tricky true/false question with four answer options, explained why each was right or wrong with citations to course materials, then fed the question to an AI tool and evaluated whether the AI got it right. Students who found a question the AI answered incorrectly had to explain what the AI got wrong and provide a screenshot. This turned assessment into a learning activity and gave AI literacy a concrete form, because students were practicing the exact skill I want them to carry forward: evaluating an assertive claim against primary sources.

Mentoring Undergraduate Researchers

In one-on-one mentoring, I worked with an undergraduate research assistant, Jacob, beginning with acoustic extraction and annotation. Over the course of a year, he progressed to independent statistical modeling and experimental design, eventually leading a collaborative project to a presentation at the Acoustical Society of America. He has since entered a Ph.D. program at UC Davis. I also guided pairs of students through replicated experiments in an experimental methods seminar, from preregistration through data analysis to in-class presentation.

Courses Prepared to Teach

I am prepared to teach introductory psychology, research methods, statistics for the behavioral sciences, cognitive psychology, and upper-division courses in perception or language. I have served as instructor of record for language acquisition (cross-listed in Language Science and Cognitive Sciences), and as teaching assistant for psychology of language (twice), human memory, developmental psychology, probability and statistics, a seminar on current research in speech science, and experimental methods. In a cognitive psychology course, I plan to adapt the hands-on speech perception activities described above, where students collect their own perceptual data and measure it against theoretical predictions. In research methods, I plan to use the preregistration-to-presentation structure I have refined through mentoring undergraduates, pairing experienced students with newer teams. I am well positioned to teach psychology of language, having served as teaching assistant for the course twice and as instructor of record for a closely related course in language acquisition. I look forward to developing an upper-division seminar on speech perception and perceptual learning, drawing on my research to give students direct exposure to ongoing questions in cognitive psychology. I plan to connect coursework to students’ experiences with accent diversity and multilingual environments, and I see potential for community partnerships connecting student projects to real communication challenges through the experience-sampling tools from my research.
